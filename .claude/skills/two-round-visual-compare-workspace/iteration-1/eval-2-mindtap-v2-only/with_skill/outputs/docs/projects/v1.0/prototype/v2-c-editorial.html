On the cost of a thought

A capture pipeline is a small thing — ink into a box. Yet every step along the way takes its cut. The v1.1 build at Mindtap spent eighty-seven cents to keep a thousand notes. Here is what we did about it, and what we are still doing.

By J. Soto 14 June 2026 14 min read From the Engineering notebook

The first thing to admit about a capture pipeline is that it is, at heart, a small and humble thing. Ink goes in, a note comes out. We have done this since the Romans did it, and the design problem has not changed. What has changed is the cost of every step in between, and the cost is now something the user pays — not in cents, exactly, but in latency, in battery, in the small erosion of trust that comes from a thing that does not feel quite ready.

For Mindtap's v1.1 build, the wall-time profile was not flattering. The OCR pre-pass ate seventy-eight percent of every capture, and the per-keystroke preview render paid its own tax in jank and attention. The LLM behind the summariser was, ironically, the cheapest part. We treated OCR like a hard gate when it should have been an optional pre-flight; we treated preview as a courtesy when it was, in fact, a leak.

Treat OCR like an optional pre-flight, not a hard gate. Defer it behind the tap-to-open action, not the type-to-capture action.— Engineering log, 31 May

The first lane was the obvious one. We batched OCR calls into a thirty-second window, collapsing nineteen separate calls into two with no regression on the validation set. The second lane was the quieter one. Swapping the on-device tokenizer to the 32k BPE built last month saved fourteen percent on tokens, which trimmed the LLM cost line directly — same accuracy, fewer syllables. The third was the easiest to ship and the hardest to defend: we retired the per-keystroke preview render. Users only read the final card.

What remains, as of this writing, is the cold-start skip for empty notebooks and the EU entitlement gate that holds the tokenizer swap until the 26.2 build. The combined picture is a drop from eighty-four cents to eleven cents per thousand notes — an eighty-seven percent reduction — without any loss in the felt quality of the editor. We are tempted, on a quiet day, to call that finished. We are not, on a louder day.
